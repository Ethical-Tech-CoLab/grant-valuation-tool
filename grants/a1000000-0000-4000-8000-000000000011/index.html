Suggested title
Digital Safety Capacity for High-Risk Public-Interest Organizations

ETC proposes a two-year program delivering practical digital-safety training, threat-model assessments, and community-centered secure-tooling guidance for high-risk civil-society actors — NGOs, journalists, human-rights defenders, and anti-trafficking organizations. This aligns with the Common Good Cyber Fund's 'training' and 'protecting high-risk actors' priorities, drawing on ETC's proven strengths in capacity building for nonprofits, community and stakeholder engagement, and bridging technologists with mission-driven communities. Rather than building DNS/routing or incident-response infrastructure (which ETC does not do), we focus squarely on the human and organizational layer of public-interest cybersecurity, where our applied-research and prototyping methods add durable value. The two-year operating structure lets us stand up a repeatable, open-methodology digital-safety capacity program serving actors who are routinely targeted but under-resourced.

Key aspects to lead with

  • Training and capacity building for high-risk actors

    The fund explicitly lists 'training' as a fundable activity and prioritizes protecting NGOs, journalists, and human-rights defenders — this is the one funded category where ETC has genuine, demonstrable capability (capacity building for nonprofits and public-interest technologists).

  • Serving high-risk digital actors via anti-trafficking work

    ETC's technology-to-combat-human-trafficking focus area involves organizations and populations that are high-risk digital actors, giving a concrete, non-invented population and a credible 'direct services' angle the eligibility rules require.

  • Community-centered design and stakeholder engagement

    Differentiates ETC from purely technical cybersecurity providers; the funder's 'technology for good' and human-rights framing rewards approaches grounded in the needs of the people being protected, not just infrastructure.

  • Open-source tooling and published outputs

    Aligns with the Internet Society Foundation's ethos of shared public-interest infrastructure and the fund's 'common good' framing — ETC can commit to openly licensed training materials and threat-model templates that scale beyond the grant.

Suggested outline

  1. 1
    Organizational overview and eligibility

    Confirm 501(c)(3) status and a bank account able to receive US foundation funds. Clearly frame ETC as a public-interest technology organization and state upfront which funded activity (training / protecting high-risk actors) you are delivering — do not overclaim infrastructure work.

  2. 2
    Statement of need

    Document the digital-safety gap facing under-resourced NGOs, journalists, HRDs, and anti-trafficking organizations. Cite the mismatch between sophisticated threats these actors face and their limited security capacity — the layer ETC addresses.

  3. 3
    Program design and activities

    Detail a concrete two-year workplan: threat-model assessments, tailored digital-safety training curricula, secure-tooling selection guidance, and open publication of methods. Be explicit that ETC delivers the human/organizational layer, partnering out or referring for infrastructure-level services (DNS/routing, incident response).

  4. 4
    Target beneficiaries and reach

    Name the high-risk actor communities you will serve and how you will recruit them through existing ETC relationships in the anti-trafficking, arts, and nonprofit sectors. Give realistic numbers for a small senior team.

  5. 5
    Team and organizational capacity

    Present the senior team honestly, emphasizing applied research, community engagement, and capacity-building track record. Address the bandwidth constraint by scoping deliverables tightly.

  6. 6
    Budget and use of operating funds

    Allocate the two-year budget primarily to staff time for training delivery, assessments, and materials development — matching ETC's preference for funding applied work over overhead. Justify against the $100K–$300K range.

  7. 7
    Sustainability and common-good contribution

    Explain how open-licensed curricula and templates create lasting public value beyond the grant, and how capacity-building support from the fund would help institutionalize the program.

Maps to their criteria

  • Directly addresses the fund's named 'training' activity and its mandate to protect high-risk actors (NGOs, journalists, HRDs).
  • ETC's anti-trafficking focus area supplies a real high-risk-actor population, satisfying the 'direct services for high-risk digital actors' eligibility requirement.
  • Open-source, published-output orientation matches the Internet Society Foundation's public-interest, common-good philosophy.
  • Human-centered, community-engaged approach complements — rather than duplicates — the infrastructure providers the fund also supports, strengthening the overall public-interest cybersecurity ecosystem.

Why ETC stands out

  • Bridges technologists, communities, and policymakers — few pure cybersecurity providers bring this cross-sector, human-centered lens.
  • Applied-research rigor with published, reusable outputs rather than one-off service delivery.
  • Existing trust relationships with vulnerable and high-risk communities (e.g., anti-trafficking) that infrastructure vendors typically lack.
  • Ethics-first framing that resonates with the fund's human-rights and 'technology for good' priorities.

Gaps to shore up

  • Core capability gap: ETC does not provide cybersecurity infrastructure (DNS/routing, threat intel, incident response); the proposal must credibly narrow to training/capacity building or the application risks being scored out of scope.
  • This is a two-year OPERATING grant that 'expects an established program' — ETC has not run a standing digital-safety program, so reviewers may see this as a new initiative rather than an established one; consider whether prior related work can be reframed as an existing program or whether a partner lead is wiser.
  • Eligibility hinges on providing 'cybersecurity infrastructure and/or direct services for high-risk digital actors' — ETC must demonstrate concrete direct-service delivery, not just research, or risk being deemed ineligible.
  • Tight deadline (4 August 2026) against ETC's limited grant-writing bandwidth — assess whether the ~15% win probability justifies the effort, or whether partnering with an established cyber provider (e.g., via Global Cyber Alliance network) as a training subcontractor is a higher-ROI path.
  • Thin direct evidence: ETC should confirm it has demonstrable digital-safety training deliverables or case examples before committing; if none exist, this is a weak fit for an operating grant.
